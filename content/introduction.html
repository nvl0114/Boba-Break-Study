Simplified vs. Traditional Chinese

Which Chinese writing system should you learn?

Did you know that the Mandarin used in Mainland China and Taiwan can look quite different when written?

If you are just starting to learn Mandarin, you will probably come across two terms quite often: Simplified Chinese (简体字, jiǎntǐzì) and Traditional Chinese (繁體字, fántǐzì).

Both are writing systems used to write Chinese, including Mandarin. The biggest difference is the form of the Chinese characters, especially their strokes and structure. There are also differences in vocabulary and terminology between Mainland China and Taiwan.

So, which one should you learn?

The answer is actually quite simple:
Choose the one that matches your needs.

What Are Simplified and Traditional Chinese?

Simplified Chinese (简体字, jiǎntǐzì) is a standardized form of Chinese characters created by simplifying many traditional character forms. It is mainly used in Mainland China, and it is also widely used in Singapore.

Traditional Chinese (繁體字, fántǐzì) keeps the traditional forms of many Chinese characters. It is mainly used in Taiwan, as well as Hong Kong and Macau, although vocabulary and usage can differ between these places.

The pronunciation and meaning can be the same, while the written characters are different.

So when people casually talk about "Simplified Mandarin" and "Traditional Mandarin," they are usually referring to the writing system used to represent Chinese, rather than two completely different languages.

What Is Different Between Them?

The most obvious difference is the number and structure of strokes (筆畫, bǐhuà).

Traditional Chinese generally preserves more of the original structure of many characters, while Simplified Chinese uses simplified forms.

電 — diàn — electricity
电 — diàn — electricity
體 — tǐ — body
体 — tǐ — body
學 — xué — to study
学 — xué — to study

Some Traditional characters therefore contain significantly more strokes than their Simplified counterparts.

However, the difference is not simply a matter of adding or removing a few lines. Character forms were simplified in different ways, and in some cases, one Simplified character can correspond to multiple Traditional characters depending on the meaning and context.

For example, 后 can correspond to different Traditional forms, such as 後 and 后, depending on the meaning.

So learning Traditional Chinese isn't simply about memorizing "the longer version" of every character. You are learning to recognize a different set of written forms.

Connecting the Writing Systems: HSK and TOCFL

Your choice between Simplified and Traditional Chinese also ties directly into the standard proficiency tests you decide to take. The writing system you study determines which exam path makes the most sense for your goals.

HSK (Hanyu Shuiping Kaoshi / 汉语水平考试) is the standardized test of Chinese proficiency for non-native speakers, administered by Mainland China. By default, the standard HSK exams are administered entirely using Simplified Chinese characters. If you study using Mainland materials and focus on Simplified Chinese, HSK is the natural testing milestone to measure your progress from beginner levels up to advanced fluency.

On the other hand, TOCFL (Test of Chinese as a Foreign Language / 華語文能力測驗) is the national standardized test developed by Taiwan. TOCFL is specifically designed for learners of Mandarin and is administered primarily in Traditional Chinese characters (though test-takers can sometimes request specific formats depending on the institution, the core testing environment and preparation materials heavily center around Traditional characters and Taiwan-specific phrasing).

Understanding this connection is crucial: picking your writing system early ensures that your study materials, vocabulary lists, and practice exams align seamlessly, whether you are aiming for HSK certification for career opportunities connected to Mainland China or TOCFL certification for studying or working in Taiwan.

Vocabulary Can Be Different Too

The differences also extend beyond character forms and test standards.

So if you plan to live, study, work, or communicate in Taiwan, learning Traditional Chinese also means becoming familiar with the Mandarin vocabulary commonly used in Taiwan.

Why Are There So Many More Simplified Chinese Learning Resources?

If you search for Mandarin learning materials online, you will probably notice something very quickly:

There are a lot more resources using Simplified Chinese.

One major reason is the enormous number of people using Simplified Chinese in Mainland China. It is the standard writing system used across education, publishing, media, and many digital platforms.

Simplified Chinese is also widely used in materials created for international Mandarin learners. As a result, many textbooks, apps, websites, videos, flashcards, dictionaries, and online courses are based on Simplified Chinese.

You can even notice this with AI and online technology.

For example, if you ask an AI assistant to translate something into "Chinese" or ask it to respond in Chinese without specifying a writing system, it will often default to Simplified Chinese.

"Please answer in Traditional Chinese used in Taiwan."

If you want Traditional Chinese used in Taiwan, you usually need to specify it clearly.

This is simply another example of how widespread Simplified Chinese has become in digital environments.

It doesn't mean Simplified Chinese is "better." It is simply more common as a default because of its large user base and widespread use across education, media, technology, and online content.

"Learn Simplified Chinese first. You can learn Traditional later."

And honestly, that advice isn't wrong.

If you want access to a huge amount of learning material and don't have a specific reason to choose one writing system, starting with Simplified can be a practical option.

But you don't have to.

Do You Have to Learn Simplified First?

Not necessarily.

If you already know that your goal is to use Mandarin in Taiwan, I personally think it makes sense to start with Traditional Chinese from the beginning.

This is based partly on my own experience.

Traditional characters with more complex forms don't only appear in advanced Mandarin. They appear even in beginner-level lessons.

Because of that, learning Simplified first and switching to Traditional later can sometimes feel like learning certain characters all over again.

Of course, learning Simplified first and Traditional later is completely possible. Many learners do it successfully.

But if you already know that you need Traditional Chinese, starting with Traditional means your eyes and brain become familiar with those character forms from the very beginning, rather than having to make that adjustment later.

Traditional Chinese Looks Complicated at First

Let's be honest.

学 → 學
国 → 國
电 → 電
体 → 體

Traditional Chinese definitely looks more complicated.

But looking complicated doesn't necessarily mean being difficult to learn.

When you first encounter unfamiliar characters, every stroke can feel important. But after seeing the same characters repeatedly, your brain begins to recognize their patterns.

You don't read:

學

as a collection of individual strokes.

You simply recognize:

學 = xué

The same thing happens with words and sentences. At first, you may stop to look at every character. Eventually, familiar words and phrases become recognizable without consciously analyzing every stroke.

This is why I personally don't think Traditional Chinese is as difficult as it looks.

The biggest challenge is often simply getting used to it.

And once you're used to Traditional Chinese, the extra strokes may stop feeling "extra" at all. They simply become part of what Chinese characters look like to you.

Familiarity Can Work Both Ways

Interestingly, this can also happen in the opposite direction.

Someone who has become very familiar with Traditional Chinese may sometimes find certain Simplified characters less intuitive.

That's because simplification can change or remove visual components that are familiar to someone who learned the Traditional forms.

So the question of which system is "easier" isn't always objective.

A character can look complicated when you first encounter it and completely normal after you've seen it hundreds of times.

What your eyes are familiar with makes a big difference.

So, Which One Should You Learn?

There isn't one universally correct answer.

Simplified and Traditional Chinese are both useful.

The right choice depends on your goals.

  • If you want to use Mandarin primarily in Mainland China, Simplified Chinese is the practical choice.
  • If you want to study, live, work, or communicate primarily in Taiwan, Traditional Chinese is the more suitable choice.
  • If you're learning Mandarin simply because you're interested in the language, either system can work.

You don't have to think of one as "better" or "more advanced."

They are simply different writing systems used in different environments.

So if you already know that you need Traditional Chinese, you can start with Traditional from day one.

You don't have to learn Simplified first just because there are more resources available.

One Step at a Time

Learning Chinese characters takes time.

Some characters will feel easy. Others will probably make you think:

"How am I supposed to remember all of this?" 😭

That's okay.

You don't have to learn everything at once.

One character at a time.
One word at a time.
One sentence at a time.

With enough exposure, unfamiliar characters gradually become familiar.

So whether you choose Simplified or Traditional, give yourself time to adjust and keep going.

And if Traditional Chinese is what you need, don't let the extra strokes scare you away.
